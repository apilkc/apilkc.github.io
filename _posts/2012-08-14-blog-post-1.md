# Introduction

With a groundless set of guidelines and widespread political pomposity, we are obliged to say that the majority of our population lives in urban areas. Yet most municipalities lack basic physical infrastructure, let alone urban amenities like parks or museums. Likewise, the governability of municipal institutions is already in question.

In this complete “Mad Max” chaos of infrastructure and inept institutional and financial capacity, the term *Smart City* is coined as an urban panacea by political and bureaucratic actors.

# Unpacking “Smart City”

Let us explore the word *Smart City*, as most of us associate it with technological superiority. But before we go further, a few questions deserve reflection:

- Until now, were our cities “dumb”?
- Is smartness inherently good for cities?
- What, fundamentally, is “smart” in a city?

International literature links the idea to ICT integration aimed at making cities effective, efficient, and productive. There is no agreed-upon definition. Nepal’s National Planning Commission offers a contextual view: *“Smart city… introduction of Information Technology in public service by public participation, accessibility, sustainability along with improvement in lives of people”* — a theme echoed across global urban discourses.

# Smart City or Political Stunt?

About a year ago, the Government of Nepal proposed Smart Cities in Pokhara, Lumbini, and Palungtar, later expanding the list. During local elections, leaders from almost every party—urban or rural—expressed enthusiasm for smart city projects. Consequently, many municipalities have now allocated budgets to hire consultants for smart city reports. 

Many planners, including myself, find ourselves tangled in this chaos of unproductive planning.

# Bureaucratic Enthusiasm or Institutional Corruption?

It is not just a political rhetoric. Bureaucratic leaders are equally complicit, institutionalizing what is often corruption in the name of big consultancy projects. The bigger the project, the bigger the margin for corruption. Everyone knows you cannot invest in the *smartness* of the city if there is no *city* to begin with.

While political leaders may use it as a campaign strategy, bureaucrats must be held accountable for delivering *smart solutions for cities* — not *smart cities* as vague slogans.

# Beyond the Jargon: What Is Smartness?

Smart city, at its core, means applying information and technology to improve day-to-day governance. Some academic critiques see it as a market creation strategy by tech companies. Even if we accept the positive potentials, a crucial question remains:

**Is our society ready to adapt to these tools?**

Smart governance refers to the use of ICT to improve effectiveness and efficiency. But a city is more than digital layers — it is a physical, lived, collective space. Are our cities ready for this “marriage” between the digital and the physical? Is Palungtar ready to be a Smart City?

# Smartness Without Foundations

There are valid arguments that smart cities represent the future of urbanism. I do not entirely disagree. But current efforts in Nepal seem driven more by global rhetoric and political grandstanding than contextual urban needs. 

Integrated data systems, standard infrastructure, and capable governance are fundamental to all urban development. These do not require an additional blanket of *smartness*.

# People’s Priorities: Rethinking Urban Needs

As we evaluate smartness, we must also reflect on **sustainability** and **inclusiveness**. Our people need public toilets and drinking water more urgently than free Wi-Fi. They need broad sidewalks and clean air, not only fiber-optic cables and smart meters.

Rather than being swayed by ideological labels like *smart cities*, we should focus on **city friendliness** — livable, inclusive, humane urban environments.

Advanced cities, once caught in the industrial revolution's techno-optimism, now turn back to recover what they lost: human-scale, ecological, and inclusive urbanism.

# A Call for Contextual Urbanism

Unless we are building a utopia like *Masdar City* to capture global imagination, *smartness* should not be our top priority. Cities like Oslo, with well-established infrastructure, can afford to add digital connectivity. In our context, we need:

- **Public spaces** like parks and squares, not just virtual networks.
- **Inclusive transport systems**, before thinking of automated vehicles.
- **Honest, accountable governance**, not digital tokenism.

We need **cities filled with culture, history, and wisdom**, not urban zones burdened by technological jargon.

> Cities are to be beautiful, happy, and giving — not smart and calculative.
